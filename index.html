Can you trust the claim?

A chart can look dramatic without its numbers changing. A big sample can still miss part of a population. Two variables can move together without one causing the other. Your job is not to distrust data—it is to ask what the evidence can actually support.

Your brief

  • Separate visual framing from numerical change.
  • Inspect who a sample can and cannot represent.
  • Test a plausible third-variable explanation.
  • Write a cautious claim with evidence and a limitation.

CASE 04

Fresh application

File a cautious conclusion.

This final case is new. It combines more than one issue, so there may be several defensible limitations to mention.

SYNTHETIC / FICTIONAL

FICTIONAL HEADLINE — BrightByte Games: “New scoreboard triples replay interest!”

  • GRAPHA chart shows replay-interest scores rising from 51 to 55 on a vertical axis drawn from 50 to 56.
  • SAMPLEThe survey link appeared only on the tournament-final screen, even though the headline describes all players.
  • ASSOCIATIONPlayers who used the scoreboard more often also tended to play more sessions; session count could help explain both variables.

BrightByte Games, the headline, and every fact in this case are invented for this lesson.

YOUR CASE NOTE

Claim + observation + limitation

There is no automated prose grader. Your writing stays in this browser. The check below only confirms that you supplied each part and selected at least two issues; use the self-review and exemplar to judge your reasoning.

Which issues did you use? Choose at least two.
Scaffold: Try “The data suggest ___ in the observed group. I notice ___. But because ___, the evidence does not justify ___.” You do not need to use those exact words.
Self-review checklist + exemplar
  • My claim is no stronger than the evidence.
  • I named at least one concrete observation.
  • I explained a limitation, not just a vague “more research needed.”
  • I did not treat association alone as proof of causation.
  • I distinguished a dramatic-looking graph from the actual numeric change.
One defensible exemplar: “The fictional results suggest replay interest is somewhat higher among the observed tournament-final users, but they do not support the claim that the scoreboard tripled interest for all players. The reported score rises from 51 to 55, while the narrow 50–56 axis makes that shift look very large. Because the sample excludes other player contexts and session count could influence both scoreboard use and replay interest, a causal claim is not supported.”

Completion summary

Evidence first. Claim second.

Complete the four case checks to finish your investigation.
01Graph

Presentation changes perception; values and statistics only change when the data change.

02Sample

More observations do not repair a sampling frame that systematically misses relevant parts of the population.

03Correlation

Association can motivate questions. It does not, by itself, establish cause and effect.

04Conclusion

A strong evidence note states a claim, a supporting observation, and a limitation.

Next practice: Find a graph in a textbook, article, game stats screen, or product report. Without assuming it is misleading, ask: What is the population? How was the sample selected? What does the axis emphasize? Is the wording causal or associational?
